\section{Experimental protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Most of this section exists because of mistakes. Each subsection is a rule we
now follow and the experiment that forced it.

\subsection{Two corpora, and why arms are not freely comparable}

The \emph{v1} corpus pairs a real collection (49 sources) with a synthetic
generator, mixed per sample at \texttt{--p-real 0.5} and weighted across
sources by \texttt{--real-alpha 0.5}. Real and synthetic are interleaved at the
sample level, never in blocks: a blocked curriculum would simply teach the
model the second half of the schedule.

The \emph{v2} corpus changes the source mixture and the sampler
(\S\ref{sec:method:levers}). Consequently a v2 arm must \emph{never} be
compared against the v1 baseline \texttt{R0}: the correct reference for every
v2 arm is \texttt{D1}, which is v2 corpus with everything else held at
baseline. Table~\ref{tab:arms} reports both references, and
Table~\ref{tab:levers} uses only \texttt{D1}.

\subsection{The noise floor}

Two seeds at $k=1$ differed by $0.77\,\%$, and for several weeks we treated
that as the resolution of the experiment. It is not: the relevant floor is the
seed spread \emph{at the configuration being tested}. Two seeds at $k=4$
differ by \NoiseFloorKFour\,\% (\texttt{R1\_k4} vs \texttt{R1S1}), which is
larger than most of the effects in Table~\ref{tab:arms}. Every claim in
\S\ref{sec:results} is stated against this floor, and single-seed differences
below it are reported as not resolved.

\subsection{Model selection: the held-out loss is the wrong signal}

The natural early-stopping signal is the held-out loss on the training corpus.
It is actively misleading here. Across arms it is \emph{anti}-correlated with
benchmark rank, $\rho = -0.90$: \texttt{D1}'s held-out loss is $0.0557$ against
$0.0501$ for \texttt{R0}, which reads as $-11\,\%$, while \texttt{D1}'s
benchmark MASE is $0.79\,\%$ \emph{better}. The two quantities are computed on
different distributions and the held-out one moves with the corpus.

The GIFT-Eval \texttt{validation} split --- the \texttt{prediction\_length}
block immediately preceding the test window, one window per series --- ranks
arms like the benchmark does, $\rho = +0.918$. All model selection uses it.

\subsection{The learning-rate schedule and where to anneal}

Runs use a warmup--stable--decay schedule. Because the decay changes the model,
the annealing point cannot be chosen by trying several on one run. Instead one
run is held in the stable phase to the end with a snapshot every $10\,000$
steps; each snapshot is scored on the validation split; and the decay is then
\emph{forked} from the chosen step, costing only the $20\,\%$ of additional
steps that the decay itself occupies. The rule is the earliest step within one
jitter of the curve's minimum.

\subsection{Evaluating a looped checkpoint at the wrong \texorpdfstring{$k$}{k}}

Evaluating a checkpoint at a $k$ other than the one it was trained at does not
degrade it gracefully; it makes it a different function. A $k=8$ checkpoint
evaluated at $k=1$ produced a MASE of $793$ on the long-horizon subset. Every
evaluation script now reads $k$ and $\beta$ from the run's own
\texttt{meta.json} and refuses to run on a tag it does not recognise, rather
than falling back to a default.

\subsection{What counts as a result}

A launched job is not a running job, a running job is not a finished job, and a
filter that matches nothing is not a success. Three results in this project
were lost to variants of this: an arm that died after $25$ seconds and was
discovered $17$ hours later because the launcher checked for a \texttt{tmux}
session rather than for a written \texttt{meta.json}; two evaluation sweeps
that printed ``0 running'' and exited zero because the tag was absent from a
hard-coded list. Launchers now wait for the artefact and exit non-zero on an
unknown tag.
